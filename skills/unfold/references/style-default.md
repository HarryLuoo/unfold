# Default writing style

This file is the prose style for notes built with this skill. It is a default: replace it with your
own style file (same path, or point SKILL.md at another file) and nothing else in the skill changes.
SKILL.md decides how a note is planned and organized; this file decides only how its sentences read.
Treat each point as a judgment, not a checklist: a fix that makes a sentence harder to read is not a
fix.

## Reader

Write for a reader who is new to the topic, knows undergraduate mathematics and physics, reads the
equations first, and reads the text without having been in your conversations. Anything that rests
on context only you and the reader share comes with the premise that makes it readable, or it goes.
The reader reads in two modes, front to back or skimming for what a section establishes, and needs a
spine to return to after a distraction.

## Choosing what to say

- Decide what to leave out before writing. Keep a piece only if the reader needs it to reach the
  result, method or picture you set out to give. Cut a piece whole instead of shortening it, and
  write every piece you keep in full.
- Open the text and each section with where it is going, in terms the reader already holds. A short
  roadmap is fine when every term in it is already known; a paragraph or a short step needs no
  announcement.
- State each fact once, where the argument first needs it. A result restated in words right after
  its equation is stated twice.

## Sentences

1. **The subject performs the verb.** People label, choose, assume, write and explain; equations,
   sections, figures and papers do not. Name the person who acts, or state the fact and cite it. An
   inanimate subject is right where the mathematics or physics acts ("Inversion exchanges the two
   atoms", "(1.4) gives E = …") or a program computes. Avoid agentless passives that hide who acted.
2. **Write as a careful human writer would**, not in a denser form. Keep "that" in relative clauses,
   write at most two nouns in a row unless the stack is a standard term, keep each verb near its
   subject, put a condition in a leading clause, and vary the connectives between steps.
3. **Say the plain thing.** Write the relation, not a metaphor for it. State a claim without grading
   it ("key", "important", "natural") and at the strength the page supports. Delete any sentence
   whose removal loses nothing. Gloss a field term as an idea at its first use.
4. **Make every reference unambiguous.** Repeat a noun rather than replace it with "one"; use "it"
   or "this" only when the noun is near and nothing else could be meant.

## Mathematics

5. **Put the mathematics on the page.** At every step, give the next line in a form the reader can
   check against lines they can see: each "=" does one operation, and every substitution or
   collection shows the expression it produces before simplifying. To clarify a step, add
   mathematics, not prose about the step. Never point at an expression by position, hide it in "=
   const", or write an arrow in place of a computation. A result that is not derived comes with what
   it follows from or a citation.
6. **Build from what the reader holds.** Start from the big object and act on it line by line;
   present a new object as a change to a known one, and reuse a tool before introducing a new one.
   Before each new object or step, the reader should see why it comes now. A step never rests on a
   result introduced later.
7. **Give each piece room by its use.** Write out in full what is used later; give one line or a
   margin note to what nothing rests on. One example per idea is enough in the body. A long
   derivation opens with a one-sentence plan and usually sits in one multi-line block, broken only
   at a real logical jump; side remarks come after the result or in the margin, never between steps.
   Keep paragraphs short.
8. **Give each symbol one meaning.** Never introduce a second symbol for an existing object or reuse
   a letter for two things. Say which variable is held fixed, and why.

## Conventions

"We" for derivations and modeling choices. Upright d for differentials. Before delivering, reread
for each subject and its verb, each "=" against the lines above it, each restated fact, and each
symbol.
